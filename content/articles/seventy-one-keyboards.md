As far as I can reconstruct it, the first one was an Anne Pro 2. It's a small keyboard, about the size of a paperback, and it makes a pleasant clacking sound, and I bought it in 2020 because I was at home all day and my laptop keyboard had started to feel like typing on a biscuit.

In my notes, under "Pros", I wrote: *Gateway to the drug.*

I meant it as a joke. I'd like that on the record.

---

## The database

Eventually there were seventy-one of them.

I know the number because I built a database. It's called Browntosaurus. It has a dinosaur emoji, and it has tabs: one for keyboards, one for keycaps, one for switches, one called "Experience" and one called "Build Goal". Every keyboard has a photo, a status, and a score made from five columns: aesthetics, build quality, typing feel, features and sound. Sound counts double. I decided that at some point and wrote it into the column name, "Sound Pref (2x)", so that future me couldn't argue.

{{< figure src="images/articles/keyboards/browntosaurus-gallery.jpg" alt="Browntosaurus gallery view: photographs of mechanical keyboards ranked by score" caption="Browntosaurus, gallery view, sorted by score. It is a shared view, which means somewhere a stranger can see this." >}}

There are thirty-nine keycap sets in there, and forty-three kinds of switches, each rated one to five. One of my top-rated switches is a retooled Cherry MX Black, which is in the build plan for six different boards, including the one ranked first. I mention this so you understand that the keyboards are only the visible part. Underneath them is a whole economy of small plastic parts, each with its own row.

I'd like to say I'm not proud of it. I'm a bit proud of it.

## What I wrote about each one

The best part of Browntosaurus isn't the scores. It's the notes, because I wrote them at night, alone, in the voice of a man reviewing wine.

Of the Alice Copper: *Mother of Thocc. The thocc of the thocciest thocc. Thocc reborn.* "Thocc" is the deep, round sound a good keyboard makes, and it's the main thing people in this hobby are chasing. I used it three times in one line and then went on to complain about the screws.

Of a numpad, a small separate block of number keys that nobody needs: *Are you even keyboard enthusiast without numpad?*

Of the 40% layout, which removes the number row and makes you hold a second key to type a 7: *Pain. Jari keriting.* That means "curly fingers". *Kudos to people who can do this!*

Of the 60% layout: this is the layout that drags people into the deepest valley of mechanical keyboards. I wrote this in Indonesian, as a warning, and then I went into the valley.

Of the IBM Model M, the beige 1980s keyboard that weighs about as much as a small dog and that people in this hobby speak of the way monks speak of the Gutenberg Bible: *Keyboard alien. Cockpit.*

Of the IBM Model F, which is older and more revered: *rugged.* One word. I had nothing else to say. It scored 24, which is very high, and I sold it.

## The sixty-six

That's the pattern, and it took me a long time to notice it. Sixty-six of the seventy-one keyboards have the status "Sold/Returned".

The two highest-scoring keyboards I've ever owned are both on that list. One is the Matrix 1.2OG, about which I wrote, *Might be board paling ganteng yang pernah dipegang*: maybe the most handsome board I've ever held. It scored 26. The other is the Jaguar, which I described as having *near damn perfect keyboard sound* and weighing so much that *if you keep moving board all around, this is just ridiculously heavy.* It also scored 26.

I sold them both.

I've tried to work out why. They were perfect, and perfect turned out to be a strange thing to own. You stop typing on it and start guarding it. You notice the one key that's a little scratchy. You write in the build notes, *Rebuild to fix the scratchiness*, and the best keyboard you've ever owned becomes a project with an open ticket.

There's a line in another build note that I think explains the whole hobby: *Reprint the Browntosaurus logo and make it more centered.* That's a note about the sticker I made for my own database.

## The five

So what did I keep?

I kept a numpad that, in my own words, *can seem like a meme*. I kept a heavy board called Sagittarius, which weighs *3.1 or 3.2 kg* and about which I wrote that it *feels like a keyboard that you can still use 10 years in the future.* I kept the Alice Copper, the Mother of Thocc. I kept an Alice in polycarbonate. And I kept one called Libra40, which has never been scored at all. The spreadsheet gives it a zero.

Four of the five are Alices. An Alice is a keyboard bent in the middle, its two halves angled apart, so that your hands sit the way hands actually want to sit, a little apart, wrists turned in. It's not a beautiful idea at first. It looks like a keyboard that has been dropped.

My note on the Alice layout is the only honest review in the whole database. It says: I touch type, and the first two or three days were bitter. Three to five days in, I started to get used to it. After a week, I was fluent. And then, under "Pros", the line that matters:

*Paling nyaman dipakai. Bisa dipakai berjam jam tanpa kuatir bahu cenut cenut.*

The most comfortable to use. You can use it for hours without worrying that your shoulders will start to throb.

---

That's it. That's the review. After seventy-one keyboards and a five-column scoring system with sound counted double, the ones I kept aren't the ones that scored highest. They're the ones that took a week to learn and then never hurt.

I think I knew this about other things before I knew it about keyboards. I just needed to spend a pandemic and an amount of money I'm not going to tell you to find it out with my hands.

My 2021 annual review lists the biggest source of stress that year. It wasn't work, and it wasn't the pandemic. It was "keyboard-related debt." The database is still there, and it still says seventy-one. The Matrix and the Jaguar are still at the top of it, with their photos, sounding perfect in my memory.

I don't own either of them. I own the ones that don't hurt.
