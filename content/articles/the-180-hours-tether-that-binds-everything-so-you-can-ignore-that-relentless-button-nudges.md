**You are will and substance.**

Not a puppet string or pawn to be nudged around by every Tom, Dick, or Harry. Nudge. Nudge. Nudge.

The added wealth, the new doors, the expanding network— everything that you think would make you the king of the hill, well, sometimes they just god darn quicksand. You might mistake that pull for lifeline rope. They fling open doors to new junctions, possibilities, and -urgh– expectations. Suddenly, everything seems important, seems worth doing. If the world is doing it, why won't you?

**Breathe in. Breathe out.**

You need that pause. Don't yield. Stand firm. Not preaching Zen philosophy, but hey, Zen, if you're reading this, now you know why you got your name.

At your core is the root. The anchor. It's what stops you from becoming just another object, dancing to eternal nudge of the button.

That inner compass, the "me-ness", which when checked, has you nodding, "Ah, that's unmistakably me." Hope you can get it. Everything else—the graying hair, the extra pounds, the trending clothes—is merely the wrapping, not the gift. It may evolve in expression or you have different words for it, but you? You're always you.

Sure, there will be moments you'll feel like you've lost the plot. But in those moments, look for totem that would help you to stay root. For me, the totem looks like above. But your touchstone it would different. Of course it would different. It might be words, it might be drawing, it might be things. But this totem, you need to see it everyday. Like the thing in that Inception, but of course without the suicidal part.

---

Let me talk about the totem for a moment. Because I think most people hear the word and imagine something mystical — a crystal, a mantra, a vision board with magazine cutouts and glitter glue. No. A totem, in the way I mean it, is much more boring than that. And that's exactly why it works.

Mine is a drawing. You can see it at the top of this page. It's not beautiful. I'm not an artist. It looks like something a reasonably talented twelve-year-old might produce during a slow afternoon. But it contains — compressed into lines and shapes and a few scrawled words — everything I believe about who I am and what I'm doing here.

I redraw it every year. Not because the core changes. The core hasn't changed in a decade. But the expression shifts. The emphasis moves. Some years the drawing is heavy on work. Some years it's heavy on family. Some years there's a lot of empty space, which tells me something too.

The act of drawing it — sitting with a blank page and forcing yourself to answer the question *what actually matters right now* — is the point. The artifact is secondary. It's the process of creation that recalibrates you. Like tuning a guitar. The guitar doesn't change. The strings just need tightening.

Christopher Nolan understood this when he wrote Inception. Cobb's totem — the spinning top — wasn't magic. It was a reference point. A way to test reality against expectation. *Is this real? Am I where I think I am? Am I who I think I am?* The totem doesn't answer these questions. It prompts you to ask them. And the asking is everything.

---

Now, the 180 hours. Let me break this down because it sounds like a lot and it isn't.

**10 minutes daily.** That's it. Every morning, before the inbox opens its hungry mouth, before Slack starts its relentless pinging, before the first meeting steals your attention — ten minutes. I look at the totem. I sit with it. Sometimes I write a few words. Sometimes I just stare at it and drink coffee. The point isn't productivity. The point is orientation.

Think of it like a compass check. Sailors don't stare at the compass all day. They glance at it. Confirm direction. Adjust if needed. Move on. Ten minutes is a glance. But over a year, that's 60 hours of glancing. Sixty hours of quietly asking: *am I still heading where I want to go?*

**2 hours weekly.** This is the review. Sunday morning, usually, when the house is quiet and the city hasn't fully woken up. I look at the week behind me and ask three questions:

*What did I do that felt like me?*
*What did I do that felt like someone else's agenda?*
*What do I want next week to feel like?*

That last question is important. Not what do I want to *accomplish*. What do I want it to *feel like*. Because accomplishment without the right feeling is just motion. I've had weeks where I checked every box and felt hollow. I've had weeks where I did almost nothing measurable and felt deeply aligned. The feeling is the signal.

Two hours a week is 104 hours a year. Combined with the daily ten minutes, we're at 164 hours. Almost there.

**8 hours semi-annually.** Twice a year, I leave. Not a vacation. A retreat. One day, alone, in a different city. I've done it in Semarang. I've done it in Solo. Once I did it in a hotel room in Jakarta, which I don't recommend — too much ambient noise, too many temptations to "just pop out for lunch."

The rules are simple: no work. No email. No social media. Just me, the totem, a notebook, and whatever clarity or confusion I brought with me.

These eight-hour sessions are where the real shifts happen. The daily check is maintenance. The weekly review is calibration. The semi-annual retreat is the deep audit. It's where I've made decisions about changing careers. About ending partnerships. About starting a family. About letting go of ambitions that no longer fit.

You can't make those decisions in a ten-minute morning session. They require space. They require the kind of boredom that only comes from sitting with yourself long enough that the surface thoughts drain away and the deeper ones begin to surface, blinking in the light like creatures from the bottom of a lake.

8 plus 104 plus 60 equals 172. Close enough to 180, give or take the overflow minutes when ten becomes fifteen because you're onto something.

---

I should explain what the button is. Because I've been using it as shorthand and you might think I'm being metaphorical. I am. But also not entirely.

The button is anything that nudges you toward someone else's definition of success. The LinkedIn post about the founder who raised $50 million and wants you to know that his morning routine starts at 4 AM. The colleague who just got promoted and now you're wondering if you should have played the game differently. The conference speaker who makes you feel like your work is small.

Nudge. Nudge. Nudge.

Each nudge is tiny. Individually, harmless. But they accumulate. They layer. And over months and years, they can shift your trajectory by degrees so small you don't notice until you look up and realize you're heading somewhere you never chose.

James Clear writes about this in *Atomic Habits* — how tiny changes compound. He's right, but the principle works in both directions. Tiny positive habits compound into remarkable results. Tiny external nudges compound into a life that looks impressive on paper and feels like wearing someone else's clothes.

The totem is the counter-nudge. The 180 hours is the counter-force. Not dramatic. Not revolutionary. Just persistent enough to keep you oriented toward your own north.

---

This would help you to find your color, and express it, amidst the expectations. Its the true confidence. Find it and you will radiate your unique shade, even if circumstance try to paint you differently.

I want to be specific about what this looks like in practice, because abstract advice about "finding your color" is worthless without examples.

Last year, during a semi-annual retreat in Solo, I realized I'd been taking on client work that paid well but bored me. Not bad work. Not unethical work. Just work that used 30% of my brain and left the rest unstimulated. I was doing it because the revenue looked good and because turning down paying clients felt irresponsible.

The totem told me otherwise. When I looked at my drawing — really looked at it — the section about meaningful work was practically screaming. The section about financial security was fine. More than fine. The imbalance was obvious when I sat with it long enough.

I came back from Solo and turned down two contracts. It felt reckless. It felt irresponsible. Six months later, the space I'd created filled with work that actually engaged me. Work that used all of my brain. Work that, coincidentally, paid better too — because engaged work tends to be better work, and better work commands better rates.

The totem didn't tell me to do this. The totem reminded me what I already knew but had been too busy — too nudged — to act on.

---

The price for me to create this totem every year is 10 minutes daily, 2 hours weekly and 8 hours semi annually, in other city, undisturbed, unmoved. I'm grateful I have one, and I wish you have yours.

That's 180 hours to stay yourself. In a year of 8,760 hours, that's roughly 2%. Two percent of your time, allocated to the project of remaining who you are while everything around you tries to make you someone else.

It's the best investment I've ever made. Not the most profitable. Not the most impressive. The best. Because every other investment — in skills, relationships, career, health — depends on knowing who you're investing *for*. Without that knowledge, you're just accumulating. With it, you're building.

Build your totem. Guard your 180 hours. Let the buttons nudge.

You are will and substance. Act like it.
