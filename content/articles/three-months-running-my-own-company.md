Three months is not enough time to know whether a decision was right.

But it is enough time to know what the decision actually means.

When I decided to run my own company, I think there was a clean version of the story in my head. A brave founder leaves the familiar place, starts something new, gathers a small team, builds with taste, uses AI as leverage, gets clients, ships products, grows responsibly, and proves that there is a better way to build software.

Nice story.

Like most nice stories, it is too clean.

The real version is more mixed. More human. More coffee, overdue contracts, family dinners, morning runs that happen, morning runs that do not happen, proposals half-finished, good client calls, bad follow-ups, and the strange emotional weather of owning a company that is still forming its bones.

I am writing this partly because I want to remember the texture of it before memory polishes everything into a LinkedIn post.

This is not a victory lap.

This is a decision audit.

---

## The Good: It Feels Real

The best moment so far was not a signed contract or a beautiful deck.

It was a team gathering at KopiO.

There was me, Gita, Dion, Adit, Rijal, Hasbi, Bayu, and a few friends from SoftwareSeni. We sat together and talked about Synetica. What it could become. What we wanted to build. The kind of company we could shape if we were deliberate enough from the beginning.

I remember feeling proud. Not the loud kind of proud. More like a quiet internal click.

Ah. This is real now.

For years, the idea lived inside me as fragments. Notes. Complaints. Patterns I kept seeing in client work. Founders spending too much money building the wrong thing. Teams moving slowly because every decision needed a meeting. Products built from assumption instead of user signal. Agencies acting like delivery vendors when what clients actually needed was clarity.

Synetica gave those fragments a container.

The early signs were good too. Cenz Health moved fast. The pitch that we could build an impressive application quickly did not feel like theory anymore. Contracts moved. UMS Trans, Simba, Mili Academy, Icehouse, Grab, Hokben. Not all of them closed neatly, but enough moved to prove that the market was not imaginary.

That matters.

Because when you start a company, there is a private fear that maybe the whole thing only made sense inside your head. You can explain it beautifully to yourself, your wife, your friends, and your notebook. But the market does not care about your internal narrative. The market asks simpler questions.

Will someone pay?

Will someone trust you?

Will someone come back?

In the first few months, the answer was not a screaming yes, but it was not a no either. It was a real signal. Messy, partial, imperfect signal. The best kind.

And then there was the AI leverage.

This part genuinely surprised me. I expected AI to help. I did not expect it to change the operating model so quickly. Decks that used to take days could be drafted in hours. Blog articles could be expanded, cleaned, and deployed in batches. Internal playbooks could be produced faster than I could previously coordinate the meetings to discuss them.

The "20 people with 5x leverage" idea still sounds ridiculous when said out loud. But it feels less ridiculous after watching what one focused person with the right agents can do in a day.

AI does not remove the work.

It changes where the work is.

The work moves from typing to deciding. From producing to directing. From doing every small thing yourself to setting the shape, reviewing output, correcting taste, and making the next decision.

That is good news for a small company.

It is also dangerous news, but I will get to that later.

## The Other Good: I Still Have A Life

One of my fears about starting a company was that ambition would eat the rest of my life.

It still might, if I let it.

But the first three months showed me that it does not have to.

The 6-9 PM family window survived more often than I expected. Not perfectly. But enough. There were dinners, mall walks with Gita, the kids' activities, Zen's science competition, Mario Party, little conversations in the car, Zia being Zia. These are not side quests. They are the point.

I need to keep saying that because the company will always create a convincing reason to steal more.

One more proposal.

One more follow-up.

One more system improvement.

One more "this is important."

Everything in a young company is important. That is the trap. If everything is important, family becomes negotiable. Health becomes negotiable. Sleep becomes negotiable. And once those become negotiable, the company starts being built by the worst version of you.

I have enough data now to know this is not philosophical.

When I run, journal, and start clean, the day works better. I am calmer in meetings. More patient with clients. Less reactive with the team. More present at home.

When I wake up and go straight to the phone, the day starts leaking before it begins.

This is annoying because it is not a complex insight. I wish the solution to being a better founder was some elegant strategic framework. But the truth is more boring.

Sleep properly.

Do not start with the phone.

Run.

Write.

Close the loop.

Repeat.

Annoyingly, boringly, this is probably the operating system.

## The Bad: The Company Mirrors You

The hardest part of running my own company is not the market.

It is not the clients.

It is not even the money.

The hardest part is that the company mirrors me too accurately.

If I avoid conflict, the company avoids conflict.

If I delay a follow-up, the pipeline stalls.

If I overthink the system, the team waits.

If I disappear into research, the business gets smarter but not necessarily healthier.

If I do not create a proper operating flow, everyone else has to guess.

This is very uncomfortable.

In a larger company, you can hide inside the machine. The machine has momentum. Someone else owns finance. Someone else follows up. Someone else turns vague energy into process. Someone else makes sure contracts do not age badly.

In your own company, there is no "someone else" at the beginning.

There is only you, pretending not to notice that the thing you are avoiding is exactly the thing that needs to be done.

One weekly note said it clearly: the contracts do not close themselves. Pick up the phone.

That sentence annoyed me because it was correct.

There were weeks where the top of the funnel looked alive, but closing was weak. Contracts were 10, 15, 19 days overdue. Money was sitting on the table. Follow-ups were late. Cash flow depended on replies that should have been sent earlier.

This is one of the less romantic parts of entrepreneurship.

You do not get paid for having a promising pipeline. You get paid when paperwork, trust, scope, timing, and invoice all cross the finish line.

A warm relationship is not revenue.

A good conversation is not revenue.

A proposal that everyone "likes" is not revenue.

Revenue is revenue.

I know this, of course. I learned sales the expensive way during my SoftwareSeni years. But knowing something as an operator is different from owning it as a founder. When it is your company, every loose end has weight.

Every delay costs social capital.

Every unclear update creates doubt.

Every "almost done" that is not actually done becomes a small crack in trust.

This is probably the part I underestimated.

Not the work.

The emotional cost of open loops.

## The Worse Part: My Old Problems Came With Me

Starting a company does not make you a new person.

This is rude, but useful.

I brought myself into Synetica. All of me. The useful parts and the stupid parts.

The useful parts: curiosity, sales experience, pattern recognition, calm under pressure, ability to connect ideas, comfort with ambiguity, willingness to build systems from scratch.

The stupid parts: phone addiction, Mobile Legends, YouTube rabbit holes, too much research when action is needed, system tinkering as procrastination, and the ancient talent of making one small failure feel like evidence of a larger personal collapse.

On one low morning, I woke up, opened the phone, drifted into the usual digital swamp, and later felt ashamed of myself. Too fat. Not fulfilling potential. Letting people down. Letting myself down.

That is not the kind of sentence people put in founder updates.

But it is the truth of the first three months too.

There is a version of founder life where everything becomes fuel. Stress becomes discipline. Responsibility becomes clarity. Pressure becomes growth.

Sometimes, yes.

Other times, pressure just makes you reinstall the game.

This is why I keep coming back to running. Not because running is magic. Running is not magic. Running is boring, sweaty, and occasionally humiliating. But running gives me one clean win early in the day. It reminds my body that I am not just a brain attached to a calendar.

When running collapses, other things often wobble too.

The notes showed that clearly. Strong client weeks with weak running. Contracts moved, but health drifted. Content shipped, but the body was not anchored. The company progressed, but the founder was not always stable.

That is not sustainable.

The company needs my ambition. But it also needs my regulation.

## The Real Lesson: I Need To Work On The Business

Around May, I started reading *The E-Myth Revisited* again.

Annoying book.

Useful book.

Its central punch is simple: most small business owners are technicians having an entrepreneurial seizure. They are good at doing the work, so they start a business, and then the business becomes a prison where they keep doing the work forever.

I do not like how relevant that is.

Because I like doing the work.

I like writing the proposal. I like shaping the product. I like editing the copy. I like testing the app. I like designing the system. I like going into the details because the details are where taste lives.

But a company cannot be built only from my taste.

At some point, the work has to become a system. Not a heavy system. Not corporate theater. A real system. A way for the team to know how we sell, how we scope, how we update clients, how we escalate problems, how we decide what is signal and what is noise.

This was one of the harder realizations from the first three months: Synetica does not need me to be heroic.

It needs me to be clear.

Heroic founder mode is seductive. It feels productive. Jump in, solve the thing, unblock the client, rewrite the deck, fix the issue, rescue the moment.

Sometimes that is necessary.

But if everything requires rescue, the system is broken.

The next version of Synetica cannot depend on me being personally intense all the time. It needs operating rhythm. Weekly client updates. Clear proposal gates. Follow-up discipline. Better ownership. Better closing loops. Better separation between exploration and execution.

Less drama.

More rhythm.

Push Synetica forward, but not with panic. With rhythm.

I wrote some version of that in a weekly review, and it still feels like the right instruction.

## Do I Regret It?

No.

Not even close.

But I understand the decision differently now.

At first, running my own company felt like choosing freedom. And it is freedom, in one sense. I get to choose the method. The positioning. The people. The tools. The kind of clients we pursue. The kind of work we accept. The kind of company culture we want to grow.

But after three months, I think freedom is not the main word.

Responsibility is.

Freedom is choosing the game.

Responsibility is playing it when you are tired, uncertain, slightly ashamed of yesterday, and still need to send the follow-up.

Freedom is saying, "This is my company."

Responsibility is realizing that sentence is not motivational. It is operational.

It means if the flow is unclear, I design it.

If the pipeline is stuck, I move it.

If the team is guessing, I clarify.

If I am drifting, I reset.

If I keep choosing dopamine over discipline, nobody is coming to save the morning for me.

This is the real test.

Not whether Synetica can produce beautiful decks or impressive prototypes. We can.

Not whether AI can make us faster. It can.

Not whether there is market signal. There is.

The test is whether I can become the kind of founder who turns signal into a company.

Not a collection of heroic efforts.

Not a beautiful idea.

Not an impressive burst of energy.

A company.

Three months in, I am still convinced the decision was right.

But I am less naive about what "right" means.

Right does not mean easy.

Right does not mean smooth.

Right does not mean I become a better person automatically just because the stakes are higher.

Right means this is the struggle I choose.

And for now, that is enough.
