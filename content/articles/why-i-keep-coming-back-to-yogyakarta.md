The bridge. That's what I want to say when a friend asked why I choose to stay here.

I was four at that time.

The sky started to darken, the lights on the sideways flickering and the rickshaw transport us lazily crawling through the empty street. Few pools of water splashed, emitting a pleasant sound as we crossed that bridge. The scent of the after rain is so strong in the air and there is a stoic patience of the city. "Easy," it says. Like I don't need to worry about my destination.

That simple frame of the bridge somehow imprinted in my brain and never leave. I left the city a week later and carry on, hearing the story here and there from the distance, moved from one island to the other island within the country.

What I remember most clearly — more clearly than the bridge itself — is the sound. Not the visual, which has been overwritten and reconstructed by memory so many times that I can't trust it anymore. The sound. Water against stone. The rickshaw's creak. My father's breathing next to me, even and unhurried. The absence of urgency.

Four-year-olds don't understand cities. They understand feelings. And the feeling of Yogyakarta, compressed into that single crossing of a bridge on a wet evening, was this: *you can slow down here.* The city will wait.

---

Yogyakarta history as a city is quite well documented. 400 years ago, a commoner without a king bloodline named Sutawijaya rebelled against his foster father and build his own kingdom right in the middle of Java island. Kingdom come, kingdom go they say. There is more powerful one come before his, but nothing last more. It retains its structure even after the whole country declares itself as a republic.

In 1946, Indonesia capital moved temporarily to Yogyakarta, before moved back again to Jakarta. Later this city becomes one to the most important education hub in Indonesia but never able to grow itself beyond that.

This is the peculiar thing about Yogyakarta. It had every opportunity to become something bigger. A former capital city. A cultural center. Home to Gadjah Mada University and dozens of other institutions that produce the country's doctors, engineers, and bureaucrats. The ingredients for growth were all there. The recipe was obvious. And the city, collectively, politely, stubbornly, said: no thank you.

Some businessman and woman I know whispering their disdain about how hard it is to build a business in this city and push for changes. There is this story about the plan to build a highway to Yogyakarta that almost botched as Sultan think that small merchant wouldn't be able to sell the merchandise if the plan comes true.

The Sultan — Sri Sultan Hamengkubuwono X — is central to understanding this resistance. He's not just a cultural figurehead. He's the governor. The only governor in Indonesia who isn't elected but inherits the position. A literal king sitting inside a democratic republic, governing by tradition in an age of disruption. When he decided that the highway would hurt the warungs and small traders along the old roads, that wasn't nostalgia. That was governance. A different kind, operating on principles that quarterly earnings reports can't measure.

I respect this immensely, even when it frustrates me. Especially when it frustrates me. Because the frustration means the system is working — it's forcing you to confront whether your idea of progress is actually progress, or just velocity.

This is a city that resistant to change. And business hates that attitude.

It's not only the old guard, but the young one also think and view the same. One of the most famous slogans of the city, "Yogya Ora Didol" (Yogyakarta is not for sale) is part of the people verse and help to understand why you can't find a skyscraper building here.

---

The student culture is inseparable from the city's identity. Walk through Prawirotaman on any evening and you'll see them — clusters of twenty-somethings in too-big t-shirts, smoking kretek on the steps of guesthouses that cater to European backpackers and local artists in equal measure. They argue about politics. They play guitar badly but with commitment. They run poetry readings in someone's rented room.

Babarsari, where the design and IT campuses cluster, has a different energy. More commercial. More hustle. The internet cafes that used to line the streets have been replaced by coworking spaces, but the fundamental economy is the same: young people renting cheap rooms, eating cheap food, building skills they'll take somewhere else.

And that's the paradox. Yogyakarta is a city that produces ambition but can't always contain it. The best graduates leave. They go to Jakarta, to Singapore, to wherever the salaries match the talent. The city watches them go with the calm of someone who knows they'll be back. Not all of them. But enough.

Enough come back to open restaurants in Kota Gede that serve traditional Javanese food with a twist learned in Melbourne. Enough come back to start studios in Kotagede that design products sold globally. Enough come back to raise children in neighborhoods where the rent is still reasonable and the pace allows for actual parenting instead of the frantic logistics that pass for family life in Jakarta.

The square which located in the front of the palace is one of the most bare squares you'll ever see. No fountain, no solid paving covering it, no garden, nothing. While every year there is a celebration happening, most of the day it spends its time being a single rectangular square with two trees in the middle, and solid soil cover most of the surface.

I love that square. I love its refusal to perform. Every other city in Indonesia would have turned it into a selfie spot with LED lights and a hashtag. Yogyakarta keeps it empty. Two trees. Dirt. The occasional kid flying a kite. The occasional tourist looking confused, as if they'd been promised something more and couldn't understand that the emptiness *was* the something.

It captures the essence of the city. In the world that always changes, and technology close the distance and border, it prefers things to remain the same and accessible for the commoner.

A paradox really, as a lot of student coming to Yogyakarta and most of them are quite progressive. Yogyakarta is used to see the student antic who doing strange things to understand themselves and become very tolerant in the process. People quick to forgive if you clip them while driving, and generally doesn't care whether you are Muslim, Christian, or just an atheist.

---

The neighborhoods tell their own stories. Timuran, where the old Javanese aristocracy lives in houses with teak pillars and courtyards that haven't changed in a century. Demangan, with its university boarding houses and the perpetual smell of instant noodles at 2 AM. Kotagede, the original capital of the Mataram Sultanate, where silverwork shops operate in buildings older than any nation in Southeast Asia.

And Pakuningratan. My Pakuningratan. Where I spent six years working in a colonial building that felt more like home than any house I've ever rented. But that's another story.

Each neighborhood has its own rhythm. Its own relationship with time. The city doesn't move as one body — it's more like an archipelago of micro-cultures, loosely connected by the same roads and the same Sultan and the same fundamental agreement that speed is not a virtue.

---

It what makes me fall in love with Yogyakarta and always come back again here. I'm coming back 14 years after I cross that bridge and then left for Jakarta. I'm coming back again 25 years later when I'm a little bit older and decide to build my family and career here.

Someone who I admire says that you should not put an attribute to yourself. Detach yourself from the origin, hobby or your company. Detach all of your attributes as it would stifle progress and put a blind in front of your eyes to the further change. While I love this idea and can relate to it, the realisation that I will keep coming back to this city make me accept that Yogyakarta already become part of my identity.

In 2002, a few days after I take care of all of the university paperwork, I decided to take my time to wander around. I walk passing through the famous Tugu and Malioboro and then decided to go to east from a point called "Yogyakarta Zero". Two minutes walking and I stunned to saw it. The bridge. It's still there! I recognize it as the back of my hand. It doesn't change a single bit, become extremely loyal to the image that glued to my four years old body. I remember it when I was 4, and I started to believe it when I was 18.

I stood on that bridge for a long time. I think I was crying, though I wouldn't have admitted it then. Not from sadness. From something I didn't have a word for at eighteen. Recognition, maybe. The shock of encountering physical proof that a memory is real. That the thing you've been carrying in your head for fourteen years — the thing you weren't even sure happened, because you were so small and the world was so large — actually exists. It's right here. Under your feet. Unchanged.

The city gave me that. Not as a gift. Not deliberately. Just by being what it has always been: a place that doesn't change its bridges to make room for wider cars. A place that trusts that the old structure, the original stone, will hold.

---

More than infrastructure, a city is more deeply coloured by its content. The people here. The activities. The way they drive. The way they earn money. The way they greet each other. It fosters a feeling of particular flow for me. This is a city of the present that keeps lingering in the past tense. A place where a warm melancholy retained as it's people relentlessly trying to stop the time.

They won't succeed, of course. No one does. The malls are creeping in from the north. The ring road gets more congested each year. The angkringan are slowly being replaced by coffee shops that serve pour-overs at prices that would have seemed obscene a decade ago.

But the creep is slow. The resistance holds. And every evening, when the light does that specific Yogyakarta thing — going amber and then purple and then dark blue over the rooftops of Malioboro — I cross a bridge somewhere in the city and I'm four years old again. The rickshaw creaks. The water splashes. The air smells like rain.

Easy, the city says.

I'm still here.
